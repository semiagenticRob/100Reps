A lot of thinking lately about how AI-assisted building changes the way reps can succeed or fail — and it comes down to methodology.

When you don't spend enough time defining your architecture, product, and interface decisions *before* you start vibe coding, you bake in technical debt fast. The earlier reps suffered from this. The lesson: spend substantially more time on back-end structure and organization, define the product and front-end interface choices, and *then* write code.

The other thing I'm learning: build harder MVPs that let you vet ideas faster.

**Rep 006 — Teshuvah Read Along:** Spent a lot of time building it out, and now I'm going to have to gut it and rebuild from the ground up. We met with a collaborating rabbi who has a beautiful siddur that's quite different from what's baked into the current app — which means a full rebuild. That said, not much is truly lost. The core functionality — the read-along mechanic and continuous scroll — has been refined through the process. The foundation is solid even if the structure has to change.

**Rep 013 — Civic Transparency Tool:** Took the opposite approach. Launched the dumbest possible version — not a responsive aggregation platform, just a tool that reviews existing videos and generates a newsletter. People love it. The response has been almost immediate. It took substantially less time to build. This is the template for how MVPs should work.

**Rep 021 — n-of-one:** Built as a stripped-down version of the original stack tracker concept, not architected as a standalone app — it runs on my existing setup and is built just for me. One user. But I'm the user, and I'm learning fast. If I ever want to turn it into a broader platform, the architecture will need to change significantly for other users. But for now it validates the problem immediately, which is the whole point.

The through-line: a dumb, fast thing that proves the idea beats a well-built thing that proves nothing.
